Approach

Undergraduates routinely mistake recognizing an idea for being able to use it. Most of what I do as an instructor is aimed at that gap. I build courses around four commitments, which I think of as REPS:

Retrieval. Students should have to pull a concept out of memory rather than reread it. Assessments in my course ask students to produce an answer before they are shown one. [TBD — one concrete ECO 146 example.]

Examples first. A fully worked example, narrated step by step, comes before students are asked to work on their own. I spend a disproportionate share of my preparation on how these examples are sequenced and displayed rather than on what I plan to say about them. [TBD — one concrete example.]

Practice, spaced. Every core concept appears at least three times in different forms — in a lecture, in a problem set, and again in a later cumulative context. Spacing those encounters is what turns recognition into fluency. [TBD — name one concept and trace where it reappears.]

Scaffolding. Problem sets open with a structured version of a problem and close with an unstructured one, so that students who arrive underprepared have a way in and students who arrive ready are not held back. [TBD — one concrete example.]

Teaching a compressed six-week term sharpened all of this. There is no room in a summer session to recover a week that goes badly, which forces every design decision — what gets assessed, in what order, and how quickly I find out that something did not land — to be made in advance rather than improvised.

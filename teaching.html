Approach

Undergraduates routinely mistake recognizing an idea for being able to use it. Most of what I do as an instructor is aimed at that gap. I build courses around four commitments, which I think of as REPS:

Retrieval. Students should have to pull a concept out of memory rather than reread it. Assessments in my course ask students to produce an answer before they are shown one. Every ECO 146 class meeting closed with a five-item exercise that students answered before any solution appeared, and the same skill returned days later on the graded problem set with new numbers: the CPI conversion they first met in class as a $12 sandwich came back on Problem Set 1 as a $90 textbook rental, this time also asking whether the real price had risen. Every calculation question on every problem set and on the midterm carried the same instruction — show the condition or formula, the substitution, and the intermediate steps. Unsupported final answers received little or no credit.

Examples first. A fully worked example, narrated step by step, comes before students are asked to work on their own. I spend a disproportionate share of my preparation on how these examples are sequenced and displayed rather than on what I plan to say about them. In Class 10 I worked one example from production all the way to cost — labor requirement, variable cost, total cost, marginal cost. Problem Set 3 handed students that same chain with new numbers and closed by asking them to verify their marginal cost a second way, as w/MPL. The in-class example is what they check their own work against, and the exercises that follow it change one parameter at a time.

Practice, spaced. Every core concept appears at least three times in different forms — in a lecture, in a problem set, and again in a later cumulative context. Spacing those encounters is what turns recognition into fluency. Horizontal summation is the clearest case. Students met it in Class 8 as a wheat market with domestic and export buyers. Problem Set 2 asked them to build a two-group market demand curve and locate its kink. The midterm asked for the same construction with different demands, then added an elasticity calculation on the segment they had just derived. Problem Set 4 applied the identical logic to market supply with two types of firm. Problem Set 5 used it once more, to find the single uniform price a price-discriminating monopolist would charge. Five encounters across five weeks, each one inside a different problem.

Scaffolding. Structure comes off in stages rather than all at once, so that students who arrive underprepared have a way in and students who arrive ready are not held back. Problem Set 2 broke the market-demand problem into six numbered steps: choke prices, who buys in each price range, the horizontal sum, the kink, the graph, and a quantity at a stated price. The midterm asked for the same object in four parts, folded two of those steps together, and attached a new question at the end. Within a single problem set the same principle applies in miniature: the auto-graded items isolate one piece at a time, and the hand-graded questions require the pieces to be assembled.

Teaching a compressed six-week term sharpened all of this. There is no room in a summer session to recover a week that goes badly, which forces every design decision — what gets assessed, in what order, and how quickly I find out that something did not land — to be made in advance rather than improvised.

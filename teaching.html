Teaching Philosophy

My teaching philosophy is Teaching at the Margin. Economists make decisions at the margin: small changes, each one checked against what just happened. I teach the same way. Each letter is a practice I use in class; open a card to see how it works in my courses.

Make it concrete

New ideas start from prices students pay every day.

In my classroom

In ECO 146, students turned the nominal price of a $12 sandwich into a real price in class, then did the same for a $90 textbook rental on the next problem set. See the exercise.

Attempt first AI

Students try before they see an answer.

In my classroom

Every ECO 146 lesson ended with a short exercise that students answered before I showed the solution. In recitation I give two to three minutes of thinking time before anyone speaks.

With AI: students solve a problem on their own before they ask an AI tool, and solve a new one on their own afterward.

Release the steps

A complete worked example comes first; then I hand the steps over.

In my classroom

Problem Set 2 walked through a market demand problem in six numbered steps. A similar midterm problem gave four prompts, and students supplied the missing steps.

Get every voice

Who answers should not depend on who raises a hand first.

In my classroom

I use name cards until I know every student. After thinking time I call on students at random, and a student who is unsure can pass the question to a classmate nearby.

Inspect the reasoning AI

A score shows that an answer went wrong, not why.

In my classroom

After the first ECO 001 quiz this fall, students first worked out why they lost points, and my review started from their answers. In office hours, students explain their reasoning before I respond.

With AI: students check an AI-generated answer claim by claim against their own diagram and correct what it gets wrong.

Nudge and adjust

Small corrections, made early.

In my classroom

After a hard topic, my follow-up message tells students exactly what to review, such as the meaning of elasticity and the midpoint formula. In ECO 146, automatically graded work showed within a day where the class stood; after the midterm I gave graphing and the calculus of optimization more time and pointed students to a math toolkit I wrote.

With AI in the room: a four-step activity

In my ECO 001 recitations this fall, I used an activity on tax incidence that I built in Lehigh's SMART program. Students use AI to support their understanding while checking its reasoning themselves. I plan to use similar steps in my Spring 2027 course.

  1. 1Solve without AIDraw and label a tax-incidence diagram and explain who bears more of the tax.
  2. 2Check the AIAnnotate an AI answer claim by claim against the diagram, correct its errors, and compare with a partner.
  3. 3Sharpen the promptRevise the prompt so the AI must use relative elasticity and a labeled diagram, then compare the two answers.
  4. 4Transfer without AISolve a new scenario alone first, then explain where an AI answer agrees or disagrees.

The activity is low stakes. When students use their own AI tool, they record the tool, their prompts, and how they checked the answer. MARGIN draws on practices from ACUE's Effective Teaching Practices modules and Lehigh's SMART program; my teaching statement (PDF) says more.

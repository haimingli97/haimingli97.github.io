Approach

Undergraduates routinely mistake recognizing an idea for being able to use it. Most of what I do as an instructor is aimed at that gap. I design courses around four commitments, which I think of as REPS:

Retrieval. Students should have to pull a concept out of memory, not reread it. Assessments in my course ask students to produce an answer before they are shown one. [TBD — one concrete ECO 146 example.]

Examples first. A fully worked example, narrated step by step, comes before students are asked to work independently. I spend a disproportionate share of my preparation on the sequencing and display of these examples rather than on what I plan to say about them. [TBD — one concrete example.]

Practice, spaced. Every core concept appears at least three times across the term in different forms — in a lecture, in a problem set, and again in a later cumulative context. Spacing those encounters is what converts recognition into fluency. [TBD — name one concept and trace where it reappears.]

Scaffolding. Problem sets begin with a structured version of a problem and end with an unstructured one, so that students who arrive underprepared have a way in and students who arrive ready are not held back. [TBD — one concrete example.]

Teaching online sharpened all of this. Without a room to read, an explanation has to carry its own weight, and any ambiguity in how a concept is presented shows up immediately in student work rather than in a puzzled face. That constraint made me a more deliberate designer of materials than I would otherwise have become.

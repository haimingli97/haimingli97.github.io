Approach

Scarcity is the first idea in intermediate microeconomics, and it is also at the center of my research. My papers ask what people do when access to something changes: a dispensary across the state line, an affordable car to get to work, dental coverage, a bed in a psychiatric hospital. Usually they do not simply go without. They substitute, travel farther, or put things off, and the cost shows up somewhere the policy did not plan for. I bring that question into the classroom in the first week.

I also treat the classroom as a problem of limited resources. Students have other courses competing for their time, a summer session leaves little room to recover from a bad week, and I often cannot see what is not working unless students show me. Four practices follow from this.

Practice before the answer. Each ECO 146 class ended with a short five-question exercise that students completed before I showed any solutions. The same skill came back a few days later on the graded problem set with new numbers. Students first converted the price of a $12 sandwich into real terms in class, then did the same for a $90 textbook rental on Problem Set 1 and decided whether its real price had gone up.

Worked examples first, then less support. I start with a fully worked example and then remove the steps one at a time. Problem Set 2 broke a market demand problem into six numbered steps, and the midterm asked for the same result in four. On every calculation question, students had to show the condition they used, the substitution, and the steps in between.

Come back to each skill several times. Each core skill appears at least three times, in a different problem each time. Horizontal summation showed up five times in five weeks: a wheat market in class, a two-group demand curve with a kink, a midterm question that added an elasticity calculation, market supply with two types of firms, and the single price a price-discriminating monopolist would charge.

Check my own teaching early. Most assignments are graded automatically, so I see results within a day. The in-class exercises show me misunderstandings while there is still class time to address them, and I write each problem set after looking at how students did on the one before.
